      in 1915. In 1916 Karl Schwarzschild solved Einstein's field equations for a

       clocks outside it. The Schwarzschild radius is a horizon only when the mass

## Stationary clocks deeper in the well run slower

This equation compares observers held at fixed radii outside a nonrotating,
spherically symmetric mass. A stationary clock deeper in the gravity well
accumulates less proper time than a stationary clock far away. Rotation, motion,
and the interior of an extended body require different descriptions.

For a black hole, the formal stationary-clock rate approaches zero as r approaches
r_s from outside. A physical clock cannot remain stationary at the horizon: the
acceleration needed to hover diverges. An infalling observer instead crosses the
horizon in finite proper time, and their own clock never freezes. A distant
observer receives the infaller's signals ever more delayed and redshifted rather
than receiving a signal from the horizon crossing itself.

Near Earth the effect is tiny but real. Treating Earth as spherical and
nonrotating, a clock stationary on the ground runs a hair slower than one at a
larger fixed radius because it sits deeper in Earth's gravity. GPS calculations
also include Earth's rotation and the satellites' motion. This is the
opposite-sign partner to the speed effect on the{' '}
<a href={`${import.meta.env.BASE_URL}viz/relativity/time-dilation/`}>special-relativity
time-dilation page</a>: motion slows a satellite's clock, while its weaker gravity
speeds it up, and engineers account for both.

<details class="misconception">
  <summary>Would you feel your own clock slow down?</summary>

  No. Your own clock always ticks normally along your worldline. For the
  stationary observers modeled here, the slowing is a comparison between clocks
  held at different radii. An infalling observer is not stationary and this
  equation does not describe their clock rate; they cross a black-hole horizon in
  finite proper time without seeing their own clock freeze.
</details>
