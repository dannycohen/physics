## A constant fraction, not a constant amount

The defining feature of exponential decay is that the same *expected fraction*
vanishes in every equal stretch of time, no matter how much is there to begin with.
Pick the half-life: after that long, half of a continuous amount or half of the
expected population remains. Wait the same time again and the expected fraction is
a quarter. Again, an eighth. The expected amount lost each step shrinks because
there is less to lose, but the fractional decay rate never changes.

That is why the smooth curve falls fast at first and then trails off, hugging the
axis without reaching zero at any finite time. A finite radioactive sample behaves
differently: its atom count is an integer that changes at random, and its final atom
eventually decays with probability one. The curve gives the expected fraction across
many equivalent samples, not a promise that every individual sample retains atoms.

## Half-life versus mean lifetime

Two numbers describe the same curve. The half-life t½ is the time for half of what
is present to disappear. The mean lifetime τ is the average time a single item
survives, and it is the time constant in the tidy form N = N₀·e^(−t/τ). They are not
the same: τ = t½ / ln2 ≈ 1.44·t½, so the average life is a little longer than the
half-life. After one mean lifetime a fraction 1/e (about 37%) is left, not half.

This one shape governs far more than radioactivity. A capacitor discharging through
a resistor (the RC circuit) loses voltage the same way, with τ = RC. A drug clears
from the bloodstream on the same curve, which is why doctors talk about its half-life.
Same maths, different N₀ and τ.

<details class="misconception">
  <summary>After two half-lives, is it all gone?</summary>

  The smooth exponential predicts 50% after one half-life, 25% after two, and
  12.5% after three. Those are expected fractions, not guaranteed integer counts.
  A particular finite sample might already contain no undecayed atoms after two
  half-lives, or it might contain more than one quarter of its starting count. Its
  last atom eventually decays; only the continuous or expected curve keeps halving
  toward zero without reaching it at a finite time.
</details>
